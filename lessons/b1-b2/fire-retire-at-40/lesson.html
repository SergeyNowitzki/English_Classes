Can You Retire at 40?

A B1+/B2 lesson inspired by the FIRE movement: Financial Independence, Retire Early.
Level: B1+ / B2Time: 75–90 minutesSkills: reading, vocabulary, speaking, writingTopic: money & lifestyle

1. Lesson goals

By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:

  • understand the main ideas of a text about financial independence and early retirement;
  • use vocabulary connected with saving, spending, investment and lifestyle choices;
  • discuss advantages and disadvantages of long-term financial planning;
  • express and support an opinion using useful discussion phrases.
Source inspiration: Harvey Jones, “Meet the people trying to save enough to retire by 40,” The Guardian, 18 August 2019.

2. Warm-up

Discuss these questions with a partner.

  1. What does “retirement” mean to you?
  2. At what age do people usually retire in your country?
  3. If you suddenly had enough money not to work, would you stop working completely?
  4. Which is easier for you: saving money or spending money?
  5. What do people often spend money on even when they do not really need to?
  6. Would you accept a simpler lifestyle today in order to have more freedom in the future?
Useful phrase: “I’d be willing to ___ if it meant I could ___ later.”

3. Key vocabulary

financial independence
having enough money or income to live without depending on a regular job
frugal
careful about spending money; avoiding unnecessary expense
take-home pay
money you receive after tax and other deductions
spending habits
the typical way a person uses their money
set aside
to keep money for a particular future purpose
live off
to use something, especially money, as your main means of support
investment portfolio
a collection of investments owned by a person
withdrawal
money taken out of an account or investment
return
the profit or income earned from an investment
recurring bills
regular expenses that appear repeatedly, such as rent or insurance
within reach
possible or achievable
trade-off
a situation in which you accept a disadvantage to gain an advantage

4. Reading — The FIRE idea

Before you read: FIRE stands for Financial Independence, Retire Early. Predict three things people might need to change in order to retire much earlier than normal.

Most people expect to work for several decades before retirement. Supporters of the FIRE movement challenge that assumption. Their goal is to become financially independent much earlier by combining high levels of saving, controlled spending and long-term investing.

A central idea is simple: the less money you need to maintain your lifestyle, the less money you need to become financially independent. This is why many FIRE followers try to avoid “lifestyle inflation” — the tendency to spend more as soon as income rises. Instead of immediately upgrading their car, home or holidays when they earn more, they direct some of that extra income into savings and investments.

The Guardian article gives examples of people who organize their lives around this principle. Some save a very large percentage of their take-home pay. They reduce recurring costs, walk instead of driving, choose cheaper housing or shops, and think carefully before making purchases. For them, the goal is not necessarily to become rich. It is to buy more control over their time.

One popular FIRE guideline is to build investments worth roughly 25 times your annual expenses. The basic reasoning is that a person could then withdraw a relatively small percentage each year to cover living costs. However, investment returns are uncertain, markets can fall, inflation can rise, and unexpected costs can appear. A plan that works on paper may therefore need to be adjusted in real life.

Critics also point out that the movement is much easier for people with comfortable incomes and manageable housing or family costs. A worker who is struggling to pay rent or support children may have very little money left to invest. Others simply reject the idea of sacrificing too much in their twenties and thirties. They would rather spend part of their money on travel, hobbies, food and experiences while they are young.

Supporters respond that FIRE does not always mean stopping work forever. Financial independence can also mean being able to leave a stressful job, work fewer hours, change careers, start a business, study, or choose more meaningful work without depending entirely on the next salary.

The most useful question may therefore be broader than “Can I retire at 40?” It may be: “How much freedom could I create by changing the relationship between my income, spending and savings?”

Reading focus: The text presents FIRE not only as an early-retirement strategy but also as a way of increasing personal choice.

5. Reading comprehension

Exercise B — Answer the questions

  1. What three broad strategies are associated with FIRE?
  2. What is “lifestyle inflation”?
  3. Why does spending less reduce the amount needed for financial independence?
  4. What does the “25 times annual expenses” guideline mean?
  5. Name two risks that could make a FIRE plan difficult.
  6. Why do critics say FIRE is not equally accessible to everyone?
  7. Why might some people prefer not to save extremely aggressively?
  8. Besides retiring, what other choices can financial independence create?
  1. Saving a large part of income, controlling spending, and investing for the long term.
  2. Increasing your spending when your income increases.
  3. A cheaper lifestyle requires less annual income to support it.
  4. The target is investments approximately 25 times larger than annual spending.
  5. Examples: falling markets, inflation, unexpected expenses, or lower-than-expected returns.
  6. People with low incomes, high rent, children or other major expenses may have little money available to save.
  7. They may value travel, hobbies, social activities and other experiences while they are young.
  8. Changing jobs, working less, studying, starting a business or choosing more meaningful work.

Exercise G — Discussion questions

  1. Is retiring at 40 a realistic goal for many people, or only for a small minority?
  2. Is extreme saving a form of freedom, or can it become another kind of restriction?
  3. Would you rather have a high-paying stressful job for 15 years or a lower-paying enjoyable job for 35 years?
  4. Which expenses improve quality of life enough to be worth the money?
  5. Do people buy too many things they do not need?
  6. Can meaningful work be more important than retirement?
  7. How do children, housing costs and health expenses change the FIRE calculation?
  8. Would FIRE be easier or harder in your country than in the UK? Why?

8. Mini case study

Imagine: Alex earns €3,000 net per month. Alex currently spends €2,500 and saves €500. Alex wants more financial freedom within ten years.

Task

In groups, suggest a realistic plan. Decide:

  • which expenses Alex could reduce;
  • how much Alex could aim to save each month;
  • what trade-offs Alex would need to accept;
  • which expenses should not be cut;
  • whether Alex’s goal should be “retire early” or simply “work less / have more choice.”
Challenge: Use at least six words or phrases from the Key Vocabulary section.

9. Writing task

Write 180–220 words on this question:

“Is it better to enjoy your money now or sacrifice some comfort today to gain more financial freedom later?”

Include:

  • a clear introduction;
  • at least one argument for each side;
  • your own position;
  • at least five expressions from this lesson.

10. Teacher notes / optional extension

Suggested flow: Warm-up (10 min) → Vocabulary (15 min) → Reading (15 min) → Comprehension (10 min) → Lexical exercises (15 min) → Discussion / case study (15–20 min).

Homework option: Students read the original Guardian article and identify three ideas or details that were not included in the adapted reading. They then prepare a 2-minute summary.

Follow-up vocabulary: emergency fund, disposable income, compound growth, pension contribution, cost of living, passive income, budget, mortgage, debt, index fund.
